The Underestimated AI Revolution: Agentic AI

By David Zonneveld Michel

I. The Actual AI Revolution

Most people think the AI revolution is all about generative AI: AI generating text, images, videos. Helping them write e-mails, producing high-quality content to publish online, producing advertisements for their brands in a matter of seconds. While these technologies are definitely going to have a tremendous impact on how people interact with the world, that's just the tip of the iceberg. The actual revolution is about something much bigger.

The actual revolution is about agentic AI: AI that makes decisions. It doesn't just generate useful content — it can take decisions to actually solve problems. Almost nobody is talking about it at the moment, but it's already here. Soon there will be machines managing your whole business automatically: placing orders, finding partners, negotiating contracts, filing taxes. Generative AI is really only a small part of what AI is capable of.

I'm a software developer. I see it every day — I'm actually using it right now to run my servers — and I believe the enterprises that understand these tools are the ones that will have the biggest competitive advantage in the not-so-distant future.

II. The Shift to Agentic Execution

The difference is straightforward: with generative AI you give instructions, the AI analyses them, and generates a response to give you whatever you asked for. With agentic AI it's different: you give a general order, the agentic AI tries to execute that order, and if something is missing it automatically figures out what it can do to solve it.

In the development world, for example, you mention a bug. It reads the code, executes it, confirms the bug, tries to identify the root cause based on the code, reads the state of your database, reads the environment. It detects that some variable is wrongly read, rewrites your code to test the fix, and reruns the program. It doesn't work, so it keeps iterating until it does.

Server configuration works the same way: you give the AI the program and tell it to run it on a server. It identifies what needs to be installed, installs it, starts the required services, creates a database and fills it with initial data, tries to run the program, fails, reads the error, corrects it, and keeps iterating until it works. A single instruction can have your whole server up and running in minutes — what would have taken an expert at least a day.

This is enormous, and most business owners are still unaware of it. Most people, including high-ranking ones, still think of AI as a productivity tool — making whatever they were already doing faster. They don't realize they'll soon be able to give AI full control over parts of the organization and have it make decisions, not just suggest input. It will soon manage whole departments with little outside interference.

This obviously comes with risks, too. What if the AI makes a bad decision? What if it overlooks the interests of an important stakeholder? What if the decision has unintended consequences? Most businesses are unprepared for these risks. As risky as it is to let this opportunity pass by, it might be even riskier to implement it without a thorough analysis of the consequences. I once ran into a logic bug I'd fixed, only to have an agent reintroduce it — it was working from an outdated model of the system and didn't know what I'd removed was a bug. Trusting these systems fully is a mistake in its own right.

III. The Premium on Judgment

Human judgment is still invaluable. The architecture of a system still has to come from a person — I got far better results when I understood exactly what I wanted built and asked for it specifically, rather than giving generic commands.

I also found that agentic tools which ask for human confirmation before acting make faults much easier to catch before they propagate. It's similar to a technique developers already use called code review: someone writes a feature, but before it reaches production it's reviewed and approved by someone else. Here, the "developer" is the AI and the "reviewer" is a human. It's far faster to review a feature than to build one from scratch, which makes this a good compromise between full automation and fully manual development.

I think this point is underestimated in current AI discussions. The focus is always on what AI can do and how it improves throughput — but people should also be asking how to make it dependable. There's still a lot to investigate before we get to a fully autonomous AI business.

IV. AI in the Physical Supply Chain

So far I've focused on AI in software, because that's what I know best. But the same principles that apply to development, deployment, and server management apply to more physical domains too — supply chain, ordering, and organization management.

Just as in software, supply chain AI today is mostly generative: people ask it to produce a forecast, and it gives its best shot. That's only the beginning. Soon we'll see AI managing the full cycle — automatically ordering when supply runs low, rerouting shipments when a disruption appears, responding to market volatility with risk calculations in seconds.

Take a company exporting automotive parts from China to Europe. An agentic AI could search different suppliers, interact with their APIs, compare prices, estimate landed cost and time of arrival based on disruptions and supplier trustworthiness, calculate margins across options, and decide which suppliers and intermediaries to use — then follow up on delivery. The China–Europe corridor is a particularly good example: two economies with very different laws, customs, and data formats, which is a major source of delay today. Agentic AI is well suited to bridging exactly that kind of gap.

V. The Human Architect

A human expert is still essential — just focused less on the details of each transaction and more on supervision: confirming things are working as expected and stepping in when they're not.

And while this sounds futuristic, the technology already exists. I use agentic tools in software development, maintenance, and deployment today. The reason it isn't yet widespread in manufacturing isn't that the technology doesn't exist, but that it's harder to implement: most of these tools were built by developers, and it's easier for a developer to solve another developer's problem than a real-world one they have little experience with. A rare combination of technical and business skills is needed to close that gap, and that's where I intend to be operating.

Adoption also requires trust. Someone has to stand behind the decisions these agents make, and take responsibility when something doesn't go as planned. That's something no system can replace — a human has to still be at the front.

VI. Conclusion

Agentic AI is moving from development environments into every part of a business. It will soon make day-to-day operational decisions as well as solve problems — but a human will always be needed to monitor and guide it. Without that human component it can do more harm than good, which is exactly why understanding these systems, and their limits, matters so much right now. The tools for this shift already exist. The only question left is who moves first.
